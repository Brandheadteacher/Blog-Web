Svijet reklame — AI & marketing consulting

AI može pisati kao ja. Ali ne može odlučiti što je moje

AI Can Write Like Me. But It Can't Decide What's Mine

AI može napisati rečenicu. Potpis ispod nje još uvijek morate staviti sami.

AI can write the sentence. You still have to put your name under it.

Branimir Zoraja 20. rujna 2026.September 20, 2026 9 min čitanja9 min read

Ovog ljeta napravio sam eksperiment. Devet tjedana objavljivao sam na svom LinkedIn profilu članke o umjetnoj inteligenciji u marketingu i brend menadžmentu.

U njima sam se uglavnom bavio istim pitanjem iz različitih kutova.

AI može analizirati milijune marketinških podataka. Ali može li im odrediti značenje? Može proizvesti tisuće inovacija. Ali zna li koja zaslužuje postojati? Može optimizirati product, price, place i promotion. Ali ima li princip koji ih drži zajedno? Može upravljati sve većim dijelom marketinškog sustava. Ali tko određuje pravila po kojima taj sustav radi? Može analizirati što kupac radi. Ali razumije li što ga pokreće? Može napraviti tisuću oglasa prije nego što brend menadžer popije prvu jutarnju kavu. Ali zna li zašto bi ijedan od tih oglasa trebao postojati? Može čak zamijeniti brend menadžera. Ali zna li voditi brend? A kad bude vodio brend, tko će ga naučiti što je dobro za brend?

Na kraju mi je još ostalo neugodno pitanje koje sam cijelo vrijeme postavljao drugima.

Može li AI pisati na LinkedInu kao ja? I još važnije: ako može, što onda zapravo znači kao ja?

Tko je ovo napisao?

Jesam li prethodnih devet tjedana članke napisao ja?

Da.

Je li ih napisao AI?

Da.

Ako vam odgovor izgleda kontradiktorno, dobrodošli u budućnost autorskog rada.

Problem s pitanjem „Tko je ovo napisao?“ jest što je u eri AI-ja postalo otprilike jednako korisno kao pitati tko je napravio Coca-Colu: čovjek koji je smislio recept, radnik koji je uključio stroj ili stroj koji je napunio bocu?

Svi su nešto napravili. Ali nisu napravili isto.

Nakon šest mjeseci edukacije o umjetnoj inteligenciji napravio sam chatbot koji sam nazvao Feat by Brandheadteacher i počeo ga trenirati.

Ne onako kako se trenira kućni ljubimac. Više onako kako se vodi brend.

S jasno definiranim pozicioniranjem. S odlukom za koga postoji, što obećava i zbog čega bi ga netko trebao slušati. Ali i s kriterijem što pripada meni, a što je samo njegova zgodna ideja. S razlikovanjem onoga što dugoročno gradi moj stručni autoritet od onoga što samo kratkoročno gradi percepciju. S odbacivanjem svake dobre ideje koja mi ne pripada. S ponavljanjem istih temeljnih teza toliko dugo da ih čitatelji napokon počnu povezivati sa mnom. S disciplinom da ne potrčim za svakim trendom, novom platformom ili idejom samo zato što je nova. S dovoljno strpljenja da tekst ne mijenjam svaki put kad on kaže: „Mogu napraviti i bolju verziju.“

Nisam ga učio samo kako pišem. Učio sam ga što prihvaćam. Što odbacujem. Što smatram argumentom, a što ukrasom. Kada primjer nešto dokazuje, a kada se samo pravi važan. Kada citat pojačava misao, a kada prikriva da misli nema. Kada treba objasniti.

A kada je dovoljno napisati:

Ma nemoj!

Učio sam ga da Brandheadteacher ne mora imati mišljenje o svemu. Ali kad ga ima, mora imati razlog.

Ne mora uvijek biti u pravu. Ali mora znati zašto nešto tvrdi.

Jer cilj nije proizvesti tekst. Cilj je proizvesti tekst iznad kojeg bih stavio svoje ime.

Najteže je stroju objasniti što za vas znači „bolje“

Kad bi bio brz, nisam ga nagradio novim zadatkom. Tražio sam da bude precizniji.

Kad bi proizveo deset varijanti, nisam se divio produktivnosti. Tražio sam razlog zašto bi ijedna trebala preživjeti.

Kad bi pronašao tri argumenta za moju tezu, tražio sam četvrti protiv nje. Kad bi pokušao ublažiti zaključak, vraćao sam mu ga dok nije ponovno imao zube. Kad bi zaključak bio očit, tražio sam neugodniji.

Kad bi mi pokušao dokazati da sam u pravu, podsjetio sam ga da mi za to ne treba umjetna inteligencija.

Dovoljno mi je trideset godina iskustva i malo ega.

Kad bi mi ponudio best practice, pitao sam za koga je best, u kojoj kategoriji i u kojim okolnostima prestaje biti best.

Kad bi ubacio poznati citat, pitao sam dokazuje li nešto ili samo pokušava uvjeriti čitatelja da smo obojica pametniji nego što tekst pokazuje.

Kad bi tekst zvučao previše kao ja, pitao sam je li počeo kopirati moje fore umjesto načina razmišljanja.

Kad bi mi rekao da je sada potpuno razumio moj stil, znao sam da još nije.

A kad bi napisao nešto što bi bez problema mogao objaviti bilo koji brend menadžer na LinkedInu, tražio sam da počne ponovno.

Konačno, nakon svakog članka ostalo bi u pravilu samo jedno pitanje:

Bi li ovo mogao napisati netko drugi?

Ako je odgovor bio da, još nismo završili.

Stroj je dobio trideset godina materijala. Nije dobio trideset godina iskustva

Oni koji me poznaju znaju da sam tijekom tri desetljeća rada u marketingu sakupio nekoliko tisuća primjera brendova, kampanja i marketinških strategija. To je moja baza podataka iz koje izvlačim argumente, primjere i ideje kad pišem o marketingu.

Ali nije najvažniji dio.

Najvažnije je ono čega u bazi nema.

Ona ideja koju sedam godina nisam mogao provesti, sve dok je nije predložio netko dvije hijerarhijske razine iznad mene. Brief u kojem sam trebao „povećati premium percepciju“, ali bez povećanja cijene, promjene proizvoda, ambalaže ili distribucije. Sastanak na kojem su mi objasnili da proizvod mora biti lansiran jer je direktor već obećao količine. Proizvod koji je briljantno prošao istraživanje, a zatim spektakularno umro na polici. Cijena koju nisam htio prihvatiti, ali mi je Excel objasnio da moram. Forecast koji je izgledao fantastično sve dok tržište nije počelo prodavati stvarne komade. Agencija koja mi je objašnjavala da kupac „ne razumije ideju“, kao da je problem u kupcu. Kampanja kojoj smo svi na prezentaciji pljeskali, a kupci nisu ni primijetili da postoji. Ambalaža koja je osvojila dizajnersku nagradu, ali su je kupci jedva otvarali. Financije koje su me pitale zašto radim s agencijom kad sam već ja tu. Prodaja koja je htjela još jednu akciju. Uvijek još jednu akciju.

I onaj prekrasni trenutak kada sam nakon šest mjeseci projekta shvatio da je problem najbolje razumio jedini čovjek koji nije bio ni na jednom sastanku – potrošač.

Da, AI može pročitati sve što je o tim projektima ostalo zapisano.

Iskustvo pamti temperaturu u prostoriji.

Najvažniji AI alat zove se DELETE

Brandheadteacher može u nekoliko sekundi napraviti dvadeset naslova. Ja mogu dva.

Može pronaći trideset primjera. Ja mogu tri.

Može povezati Kotlera, Ogilvyja, Roryja Sutherlanda, Patagoniju, Disney i pastu za osjetljive zube prije nego što popijem jutarnju kavu.

Fantastično.

Ali dvadeset naslova nije članak.

Trideset primjera nisu argument.

A citat nije misao samo zato što ga je netko poznat jednom izgovorio.

Netko mora brisati.

Tijekom ovih devet tjedana možda je upravo tipka DELETE bila najvažniji AI alat koji sam koristio.

Stroj je proizvodio. Ja sam birao.

Stroj je kombinirao. Ja sam se svađao. Stroj je predlagao.

Ja sam uglavnom govorio:

Ne.

Previše generički. To nisam ja. Ovo nema zube. Ovo nema stav. Ovo nema miris stvarnog posla. Ovo zvuči kao webinar. Ovo zvuči kao PowerPoint koji je pobjegao u Word. Ovo zvuči kao agencijski debrief. Ovo je dovoljno korektno da nikoga ne naljuti i dovoljno bezopasno da ga nitko ne zapamti.

Daj mi primjer koji boli. Daj mi primjer iz prakse. Ovaj primjer je dobar. Ali je li moj?

Ovo je točno. Ali nije dovoljno dobro. Ovo je pametno. Ali nije zapamtljivo. Ovo je zapamtljivo. Ali nije istinito. Ovo je istinito. Sad ga učini zanimljivim. Ovo svi znaju. Zašto bih to objavio? Ovo je argument. Ja tražim stav. Ovo je stav. Sad ga dokaži. Ako je zaključak očit, traži drugi.

Sad smo bliže.

Ali gdje je kriterij?

Sad zvuči kao Brandheadteacher.

AI je trenirao mene

Najzanimljiviji dio eksperimenta dogodio se negdje usput.

Shvatio sam da nisam samo ja trenirao AI.

AI je trenirao mene.

Trideset godina možete nešto raditi intuitivno i misliti da dobro poznajete vlastiti način razmišljanja.

A onda vas stroj pita što točno želite.

Koliko ironije? Koliko činjenica? Koliko primjera? Zašto ovdje treba Ogilvy, a tamo ne treba nitko? Zašto je jedna rečenica britka, a druga samo bezobrazna? Zašto je jedan primjer dobar, ali nije vaš?

Odjednom morate kodificirati ono što ste desetljećima radili instinktivno.

Morao sam stroju objasniti Brandheadteachera. Usput sam ga ponovno objasnio sebi.

To je možda najveća vrijednost ovog eksperimenta.

AI nema moj stil. Ima pristup njegovim pravilima

AI može naučiti pravila po kojima moj stil nastaje. Ali pravila nisu stil.

Ne može iz njih postati ja.

Stil nije koristiti kratke rečenice. Nije citirati poznate ljude. Nije staviti Nike, Coca-Colu i Hasbro u isti članak.

Nije čak ni biti malo ohol prema prosječnom marketingu.

Stil je obrazac odluka.

Koji dokaz prihvaćate. Koji odbacujete. Što smatrate glupošću. Koju kontradikciju ne možete pustiti na miru. Što ćete napasti. Što ćete braniti. Što vam je dovoljno važno da ste zbog toga spremni pokvariti prirodni slijed rečenice.

Drugim riječima, stil nije samo način na koji nešto napišete. Stil je razlog zbog kojeg ste odlučili napisati baš to, a ne nešto drugo.

AI možda može naučiti gdje stavljam točku. Prst u oko još uvijek stavljam ja.

AI neće zamijeniti autorstvo. Zamijenit će osrednjost

I zato ne mislim da umjetna inteligencija uništava autorstvo. Uništit će prosječno autorstvo.

Kao što će uništiti prosječan copywriting. Prosječne slogane koji mogu pripadati banci, telekomu, osiguranju ili jogurtu. Prosječne kampanje koje su „odlično prošle na društvenim mrežama“, samo se to nekako nije dogodilo na blagajni. Prosječne buyer persone koje imaju ime, hobi i omiljenu aplikaciju, ali nemaju stvaran život. Prosječne insightse tipa „ljudi žele kvalitetu po pristupačnoj cijeni“. Prosječne strategije koje kupca „stavljaju u središte“ tako da ga prethodno potpuno uklone iz stvarnosti. I prosječne LinkedIn članke koji počnu 'nepopularnim mišljenjem', a završe mišljenjem s kojim se slaže cijeli LinkedIn.

Hvala.

To sada znamo.

AI vam daje više vremena za ono što ne može napraviti umjesto vas

Zanimljivije pitanje je što ćete napraviti s vremenom koje vam je stroj upravo vratio.

Hoćete li proizvesti četiri puta više prosječnog sadržaja? Ili ćete četiri puta ozbiljnije razmišljati što uopće vrijedi objaviti?

Ja sam pokušao drugo.

Barem se nadam.

AI mi je dao brzinu, strukturu, varijante, poveznice i neumornu produkciju.

Ja sam njemu dao trideset godina marketinga, nekoliko tisuća brendova, praktičnih primjera, dosta vlastitih pogrešaka i nešto što se ne može skinuti s interneta: dovoljno iskustva da znam da dobra ideja još uvijek ne mora biti moja ideja.

I tu se ovaj mali ljetni eksperiment vraća tamo gdje je cijela ova serija zapravo počela.

Na kriterij.

AI može analizirati kupca. Netko mora odlučiti što podatak znači.

AI može generirati inovacije. Netko mora odlučiti koja rješava stvarni problem.

AI može optimizirati marketinški miks. Netko mora odlučiti što brend smije postati.

AI može upravljati sustavom. Netko mora napisati pravila sustava.

AI može pisati kao ja. Ali ne može odlučiti što je moje.

AI-ju možete dati tipkovnicu. Potpis ipak morate staviti sami.

Možda je to i najbolji rezultat devet tjedana eksperimenta.

Nisam dokazao da AI može pisati kao Brandheadteacher. Dokazao sam da Brandheadteacher uz AI mora puno preciznije znati što je u tom pisanju njegovo.

But apparently, learning never stops.

This summer I ran an experiment. For nine weeks I published articles on my LinkedIn profile about artificial intelligence in marketing and brand management.

In them, I was mostly circling the same question from different angles.

AI can analyse millions of marketing data points. But can it assign them meaning? It can produce thousands of innovations. But does it know which one deserves to exist? It can optimise product, price, place and promotion. But does it have a principle that holds them together? It can run an ever-larger part of the marketing system. But who decides the rules that system runs on? It can analyse what the customer does. But does it understand what drives them? It can make a thousand ads before the brand manager finishes their first coffee of the day. But does it know why any one of those ads should exist? It can even replace the brand manager. But does it know how to run a brand? And once it's running the brand, who's going to teach it what's good for the brand?

In the end, I was left with one uncomfortable question I'd been asking everyone else the whole time.

Can AI write on LinkedIn like me? And more importantly: if it can, what does "like me" actually mean?

Who wrote this?

Did I write the last nine weeks of articles?

Yes.

Did AI write them?

Yes.

If that answer sounds contradictory, welcome to the future of authorship.

The trouble with the question "Who wrote this?" is that, in the AI era, it's become about as useful as asking who made Coca-Cola: the man who came up with the recipe, the worker who switched on the machine, or the machine that filled the bottle?

Everyone did something. But they didn't do the same thing.

After six months of AI education, I built a chatbot I named Feat by Brandheadteacher and started training it.

Not the way you train a pet. More the way you run a brand.

With a clearly defined positioning. With a decision about who it exists for, what it promises, and why anyone should listen to it. But also with a criterion for what belongs to me and what's just its neat idea. With a distinction between what builds my professional authority long-term and what merely builds perception short-term. By rejecting every good idea that isn't mine. By repeating the same core theses long enough that readers finally start associating them with me. With the discipline not to chase every trend, new platform, or idea just because it's new. With enough patience not to change the text every single time it says: "I can make an even better version."

I wasn't just teaching it how I write. I was teaching it what I accept. What I reject. What counts as an argument to me versus decoration. When an example actually proves something versus when it's just showing off. When a quote reinforces a thought versus when it's hiding the fact there isn't one. When something needs explaining.

And when it's enough to simply write:

Oh, come on!

I taught it that Brandheadteacher doesn't have to have an opinion about everything. But when he does, he has to have a reason.

He doesn't always have to be right. But he has to know why he's claiming something.

Because the goal isn't to produce text. The goal is to produce text I'd be willing to put my name on.

The hardest thing to teach a machine is what "better" means to you

When it was fast, I didn't reward it with a new task. I asked it to be more precise.

When it produced ten variants, I didn't admire the productivity. I asked for a reason any one of them deserved to survive.

When it found three arguments for my thesis, I asked for a fourth one against it. When it tried to soften a conclusion, I'd send it back until it had teeth again. When the conclusion was obvious, I asked for a less comfortable one.

When it tried to prove I was right, I reminded it that I don't need artificial intelligence for that.

Thirty years of experience and a bit of ego are enough for that on their own.

When it offered me a best practice, I asked: best for whom, in which category, and under what circumstances does it stop being best?

When it dropped in a famous quote, I asked whether it was proving something or just trying to convince the reader that we were both smarter than the text actually showed.

When the text sounded too much like me, I asked whether it had started copying my tricks instead of my way of thinking.

When it told me it had now fully understood my style, I knew it hadn't.

And when it wrote something any brand manager on LinkedIn could publish without a second thought, I asked it to start over.

In the end, after every single article, usually just one question remained:

Could someone else have written this?

If the answer was yes, we weren't done yet.

The machine got thirty years of material. It didn't get thirty years of experience

Those who know me know that over three decades in marketing I've collected a few thousand examples of brands, campaigns and marketing strategies. That's my database, and I draw arguments, examples and ideas from it whenever I write about marketing.

But that's not the most important part.

What matters most is what isn't in that database.

The idea I couldn't push through for seven years, until someone two hierarchy levels above me suggested it. A brief where I was supposed to "increase premium perception" without raising the price, changing the product, the packaging, or the distribution. A meeting where I was told the product had to launch because the director had already promised the volumes. A product that sailed through research and then died spectacularly on the shelf. A price I didn't want to accept, but Excel explained I had to. A forecast that looked fantastic right up until the market started selling actual units. An agency explaining to me that the customer "doesn't understand the idea," as if the customer were the problem. A campaign everyone applauded in the presentation, that customers never even noticed existed. Packaging that won a design award but that customers could barely open. Finance asking me why I was working with an agency when I was already there. Sales wanting one more promotion. Always one more promotion.

And that beautiful moment, after six months on a project, when I realised the person who understood the problem best was the one person who hadn't been in a single meeting — the consumer.

Yes, AI can read everything that was ever written down about those projects.

Experience remembers the temperature in the room.

The most important AI tool is called DELETE

Brandheadteacher can produce twenty headlines in a few seconds. I can manage two.

It can find thirty examples. I can manage three.

It can connect Kotler, Ogilvy, Rory Sutherland, Patagonia, Disney and sensitive-teeth toothpaste before I've finished my morning coffee.

Fantastic.

But twenty headlines aren't an article.

Thirty examples aren't an argument.

And a quote isn't a thought just because someone famous once said it.

Someone has to delete.

Over these nine weeks, the DELETE key may well have been the single most important AI tool I used.

The machine produced. I chose.

The machine combined. I argued. The machine suggested.

I mostly said:

No.

Too generic. That's not me. This has no teeth. This has no point of view. This doesn't smell like real work. This sounds like a webinar. This sounds like a PowerPoint that escaped into Word. This sounds like an agency debrief. This is correct enough to upset nobody and harmless enough that nobody will remember it.

Give me an example that hurts. Give me an example from the field. This example is good. But is it mine?

This is correct. But it's not good enough. This is clever. But it's not memorable. This is memorable. But it's not true. This is true. Now make it interesting. Everyone already knows this. Why would I publish it? This is an argument. I'm asking for a point of view. This is a point of view. Now prove it. If the conclusion is obvious, find another one.

Now we're getting closer.

But where's the criterion?

Now that sounds like Brandheadteacher.

AI trained me

The most interesting part of the experiment happened somewhere along the way.

I realised I wasn't the only one training AI.

AI was training me.

You can do something intuitively for thirty years and think you know your own way of thinking pretty well.

And then a machine asks you exactly what you want.

How much irony? How many facts? How many examples? Why does this spot need an Ogilvy quote and that one needs nobody? Why is one sentence sharp and the other just rude? Why is one example good, but not yours?

Suddenly you have to codify what you've been doing instinctively for decades.

I had to explain Brandheadteacher to the machine. Along the way, I explained him to myself again too.

That might be the greatest value of this entire experiment.

AI doesn't have my style. It has access to its rules

AI can learn the rules by which my style comes together. But rules aren't style.

They can't turn it into me.

Style isn't using short sentences. It isn't quoting famous people. It isn't putting Nike, Coca-Cola and Hasbro in the same article.

It isn't even being a little condescending about average marketing.

Style is a pattern of decisions.

Which evidence you accept. Which you reject. What you consider stupid. Which contradiction you can't leave alone. What you'll attack. What you'll defend. What matters enough to you that you're willing to break the natural flow of a sentence over it.

In other words, style isn't just the way you write something. Style is the reason you decided to write that particular thing, and not something else.

AI might be able to learn where I put the full stop. I'm still the one who puts the finger in the eye.

AI won't replace authorship. It'll replace mediocrity

Which is why I don't think artificial intelligence destroys authorship. It'll destroy average authorship.

Just as it'll destroy average copywriting. Average taglines that could belong to a bank, a telecom, an insurer, or a yoghurt. Average campaigns that "did great on social," except somehow that never quite happened at the till. Average buyer personas with a name, a hobby and a favourite app, but no actual life. Average insights along the lines of "people want quality at an affordable price." Average strategies that "put the customer at the centre" by first removing them entirely from reality. And average LinkedIn articles that open with an "unpopular opinion" and end with an opinion the whole of LinkedIn agrees with.

Thank you.

We know that now.

AI gives you more time for what it can't do instead of you

The more interesting question is what you'll do with the time the machine just gave back to you.

Will you produce four times more average content? Or will you think four times harder about what's even worth publishing?

I tried the latter.

At least I hope so.

AI gave me speed, structure, variants, connections, and tireless production.

I gave it thirty years of marketing, a few thousand brands, practical examples, plenty of my own mistakes, and something you can't download from the internet: enough experience to know that a good idea still doesn't have to be my idea.

And that's where this small summer experiment comes back to where this whole series actually began.

The criterion.

AI can analyse the customer. Someone has to decide what the data means.

AI can generate innovations. Someone has to decide which one solves a real problem.

AI can optimise the marketing mix. Someone has to decide what the brand is allowed to become.

AI can run the system. Someone has to write the system's rules.

AI can write like me. But it can't decide what's mine.

You can hand AI the keyboard. You still have to put the signature on it yourself.

That might be the best result of nine weeks of experimenting.

I didn't prove that AI can write like Brandheadteacher. I proved that, with AI, Brandheadteacher has to know far more precisely what in that writing is actually his.

But apparently, learning never stops.
