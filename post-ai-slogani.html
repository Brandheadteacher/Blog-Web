Svijet reklame — AI & marketing consulting

AI može napisati 10.000 slogana. Šefov šofer sada ima kreativnu agenciju u džepu

AI Can Write 10,000 Taglines. The Boss's Driver Now Has a Creative Agency in His Pocket

Deset tisuća slogana nije problem. Problem je — tko će izabrati jedan.

Ten thousand taglines aren't the problem. The problem is choosing one.

Branimir Zoraja 30. kolovoza 2026.August 30, 2026 7 min čitanja7 min read

U prethodnim člancima rekli smo da 4P nisu četiri odjela, nego četiri verzije istog obećanja. Product kaže što dajemo. Price koliko mislimo da to vrijedi. Place gdje očekujemo da nas kupac pronađe. Promotion zašto bi itko, usred sve te tržišne galame, trebao podignuti glavu i obratiti pozornost.

I upravo bi zato Promotion trebao biti prirodno vlasništvo marketinga i brend menadžera koji upravlja cijelim marketinškim miksom.

Ne zato što marketing ima monopol na pamet. Nego zato što netko mora imati monopol na odgovornost.

Međutim, nema tog područja marketinga u kojem sam tijekom karijere čuo više diletantskih bedastoća kao u promociji, posebno kad se radilo o logu, boji, ambalaži, nazivu i sloganu. Od vlasnika, direktora izvoza, ljudi iz prodaje i računovodstva, preko ključnog kupca i trogodišnje kćeri ključnog kupca, pa sve do šefova šofera – svi bi se osjećali pomalo kreativnim direktorima.

Promocija je, izgleda, jedino vlasništvo marketinga u koje svi ostali imaju pravo ulaska bez kucanja.

Do jučer je njihov kreativni proces ipak imao prirodno ograničenje. Netko bi rekao: „Meni je logo premalen.“ Drugi bi dodao: „Meni se ne sviđa boja.“ Treći bi primjetio: „Zašto ne bismo dodali još jednu riječ da se bolje objasni?“ Netko bi s pola glasa natuknuo: „Može li proizvod biti malo više premium?“ Onda bi došao onaj koji nije bio na sastanku pa bi rekao: „Nedostaje mi nešto što govori tradiciju.“ Ili: „Zašto je ambalaža tako prazna?“ Direktor bi rekao: „Zašto ne napišemo da smo najbolji?“, pa dodao: „Moja kći kaže da bi ovo bolje izgledalo u crnoj.“ Vlasnikov zet bi se nadovezao: „Vlasniku se sviđa ono što radi konkurencija.“ Kad bi napokon sve bilo definirano, prodaja bi se pobunila: „Kupac je predložio da dodamo srce.“ „Zašto naziv nije razumljiv na svim tržištima?“ „Meni ovo zvuči previše marketinški.“ „Meni ovo uopće ne zvuči marketinški.“ „Ajmo još jednu verziju, čisto da imamo izbor.“

Danas više nema.

Danas će šofer, dok čeka svog direktora, iz dosade otvoriti ChatGPT, ubaciti prijedlog marketinga i napisati: „Daj mi deset boljih slogana.“ Za trideset sekundi već će tužakati šefu: „Evo. AI kaže da može bolje.“ Ili: „Vidiš, jesam ti rekao da ovi iz marketinga ništa ne rade.“

Ajoooj.

Problem je što će često biti u pravu.

AI je, izgleda, prokleto dobar u brendingu. Barem u onom njegovu dijelu koji se svodi na riječi i vizuale

Jedno istraživanje reklamnih slogana iz 2025. posebno je dirnulo naš kreativni ego. Slogani koje je napravio ChatGPT ocijenjeni su kvalitetnijima – novijima, smislenijima i uvjerljivijima – od ljudskih. Ali bili su manje raznoliki. AI je posebno pomagao ljudima koji su inače bili manje kreativni (Chat-GPT4 Does Enhance Creativity. But Human Ego Can Hamper its Potential).

Prevedeno na marketinški jezik: AI neće svakog šofera pretvoriti u Davida Ogilvyja. Ali će mu dramatično podići kreativni prosjek.

To mijenja odnose moći. Jer prije ste za loše ime ili slogan trebali barem nešto samopouzdanja i dovoljno visoku funkciju. Danas trebate prompt.

I zato je najveća opasnost AI-ja u promociji upravo njegova najveća prednost: može proizvesti beskonačan broj dovoljno dobrih rješenja.

Deset slogana. Sto. Tisuću. Deset tisuća.

Marketinškom diletantu, naprotiv, dobra ideja najčešće dolazi sama. Često na neobičnom mjestu. I još neobičnijim povodom.

U kupaonici. Na autoputu. Na team buildingu. Od direktora koji je sanjao slogan. Od njegovog nećaka koji „radi nešto s TikTokom“. Od prodavača koji je krivo izgovorio naziv proizvoda. Od distributera koji je rekao: „Kod nas to nitko neće razumjeti.“ Ili kad se samo žele odužiti dugogodišnjem kupcu sladoleda.

Marketinški cinik mogao bi ionako reći da je kreativni uradak neka vrsta kontrolirane halucinacije. A ako je u nečemu AI kao riba u vodi – onda je u haluciniranju.

Uzmimo nazive brendova. I prije su nazivi brendova nastajali kao današnji prompt za naming. Nije to izumio ChatGPT. Na primjer, George Eastman izmislio je riječ Kodak. Nije značila fotografiju. Nije značila kameru. Nije bila ime nekog grada, kćeri, ljubavnice ni grčkog boga svjetlosti. Nije značila – ništa. Eastman je jednostavno volio slovo K jer mu je zvučalo snažno i oštro pa je, kako je poslije objasnio, isprobavao velik broj kombinacija slova koje počinju i završavaju s K. I tako je nastao Kodak.

Danas bi taj posao AI obavio prije nego što Eastman uspije naoštriti olovku: „Daj mi 1.000 kratkih naziva od pet slova, koji počinju i završavaju slovom K, zvuče snažno, nemaju postojeće značenje i mogu se lako izgovoriti na različitim jezicima.“ Enter.

Možda bi među njima bio i Kodak. A možda još 37 boljih.

Ali AI bi riješio samo lakši dio problema: proizveo bi nazive.

Eastman je napravio ono teže. Imao je kriterij zašto jedan od njih treba izabrati.

To je razlika koju ćemo u eri AI-ja morati stalno ponavljati:

Stroj može izmisliti ime. Brend menadžer mora znati zašto će baš to ime nešto značiti ljudima.

Slogan nije rečenica. Slogan je komprimirana strategija.

Nikeov Just Do It nije nastao tako da je Dan Wieden zamolio računalo za „deset motivacijskih slogana za sportsku obuću“. Prema vlastitim riječima, jednu od inspiracija je pronašao na prilično nevjerojatnom mjestu: u posljednjim riječima američkog kriminalca Garyja Gilmorea prije pogubljenja – „Let's do it“. Wieden je asocijaciju preoblikovao u tri riječi koje Nike nosi desetljećima. Čak je i Phil Knight u početku smatrao da im takav slogan ne treba.

Zamislite taj slogan danas.

AI bi u međuvremenu izbacio još 100 alternativa: Move Your Limits. Own Your Potential. Become Your Best. Push Beyond. Find Your Power ...

Sve korektno. Profesionalno. I sve mrtvo.

I tu dolazimo do bitnog pitanja koji je povod ovom članku.

Ako sada svi mogu napraviti dobar slogan, tko odlučuje koji je pravi?

AI je divergentni stroj. On će vam otvoriti bezbroj mogućnosti za razmišljanje. Dajte mu brief: „Treba nam slogan za premium pastu za zube“ i dobit ćete 10 prijedloga. Tražite još – dat će ih sto. Promijenit će ton, ritam, metaforu, ciljnu skupinu, dužinu i stil. Ne umara se, ne zaljubljuje se u vlastitu ideju i nema problema s tim da u pet minuta odbaci 100 prijedloga i napravi novih 100.

Problem marketinga, međutim, vrlo rijetko je u tome da nema dovoljno ideja.

Problem je u tome što netko mora odlučiti koja od njih zaslužuje postati brend.

Brend menadžer mora reći: ovaj slogan zvuči dobro, ali nije naš. Ovaj je zabavan, ali ne podržava pozicioniranje. Ovaj će dobiti klikove, ali će za tri godine zvučati glupo. Ovaj je možda manje atraktivan na prvom čitanju, ali ima potencijal postati dio jezika brenda. Zato kažemo da brend menadžer mora biti konvergentni autoritet.

I što AI bude proizvodio više mogućnosti, to će vrijednost tog autoriteta biti veća.

Kad AI treba pustiti s lanca

Nemojmo sada, iz straha za vlastiti posao, poslati AI u podrum kraj printera.

U pojedinim dijelovima copywritinga stroj je već brutalan.

JPMorgan Chase još je davne 2016. počeo eksperimentirati s Persadom, AI platformom koja generira i testira marketinški jezik – od headlineova i CTA-ova do pojedinih riječi i formulacija. U testiranju su neke AI-generirane verzije oglasa ostvarile i do 450 % veći click-through rate od usporednih oglasa. Posebno je zanimljiv zaključak tadašnje CMO Kristin Lemkau: AI je pisao headlineove i copy koje marketinški stručnjak, oslanjajući se na iskustvo i subjektivni sud, vjerojatno ne bi ni pokušao napisati.

To je upravo ono gdje želim AI.

Neka proizvede 100 naslova e-poruka. Neka lokalizira copy na 37 tržišta. Neka napravi stotine CTA-ova. Neka analizira stare kampanje. Neka traži klišeje. Neka pronađe rečenicu koju ja nisam vidio.

Ali neka nikad, nikad ne odlučuje što moj brend znači.

AI bez brend strategije samo je pripravnik koji zna nešto objaviti na TikToku.

Virgin Voyages, Bransonova kompanija za kružna putovanja, razvila je AI verziju Jennifer Lopez – Jen AI. Ideja je bila da AI-avatar Jennifer Lopez osobno pozove tvoje prijatelje ili obitelj na zajedničko putovanje.

Odakle im ta ideja? Pokušajte vi okupiti šest prijatelja u pedesetima da zajedno odete na godišnji. Jedan nema novca. Drugi nema vremena. Treći mora pitati ženu. Četvrti kaže „čujemo se“. Peti ne odgovara na WhatsApp. A šesti ste vi.

Generativni AI omogućio je korisnicima da naprave personaliziranu videopozivnicu u kojoj digitalna J.Lo poziva njih šest na zajedničko krstarenje i prilagođava poruku povodu koji slave. Tako je omogućena personalizacija u razmjerima u kojima jedna Jennifer Lopez, srećom po nju, nikada osobno ne bi mogla raditi. Napravljeno je, prema vlastitim podacima, više od 25.000 personaliziranih pozivnica, gotovo 200.000 ljudi koristilo je microsite, a kampanja je ostvarila golem globalni doseg. Ali prava kreativna ideja nije bila:

„Napravimo AI verziju Jennifer Lopez.“

To može predložiti i pripravnik nakon dva sata oglašavanja na TikToku.

Prava ideja bila je:

Ako je najveći problem zajedničkog putovanja okupiti ljude, neka ih umjesto vas pozove Jennifer Lopez.

Tu tehnologija više nije ideja. Ona je izvršenje ideje.

AI je od jedne slavne osobe napravio desetke tisuća osobnih pozivnica. Ali čovjek je morao shvatiti zašto bi itko uopće želio poslati jednu od njih.

To je razlika između AI trika i marketinga. AI je ovdje bio izvrstan sluga jer je dobio dobar zadatak.

Kantarova aktualna analiza AI oglašavanja pokazuje koliko je ta razlika važna. Autori su analizirali stotine GenAI oglasa kombinirajući anketno ispitivanje s metodom facial codinga, odnosno mjerenjem spontanih emocionalnih reakcija. Rezultati su pokazali da se više od 40 % oglasa u kojima je GenAI bio prilagođen tonu glasa i distinktivnim elementima brenda svrstalo među oglase s najjačom prepoznatljivošću brenda, dok su oglasi s neprirodnim, distraktivnim vizualima kakve AI zna napraviti – u prosjeku imali slabiji brending. Kantarov zaključak zapravo je vrlo jednostavan: AI nema vaš brend „u srcu“ ako mu ga vi prethodno niste ugradili („Rethinking AI-generated advertising: how real people really react“).

Najvažnija kreativna odluka ponekad je – NE.

Pravo vlasništvo marketinga nad promocijom najbolje se ne vidi kada marketing kaže što ćemo napraviti. Nego kada kaže što nećemo.

Dove je svoj Real Beauty princip proširio na eru AI-ja i izričito se obvezao da neće koristiti slike generirane umjetnom inteligencijom umjesto stvarnih žena niti digitalno proizvoditi nedostižne ideale ljepote. Tehnologija može. Brend je odlučio da ne smije. To je brend menadžment.

Ne pitanje:

Možemo li to napraviti?

Nego:

Je li to naše?

Ogilvy je upozoravao da potrošač nije moron i da ga nećete uvjeriti sloganom i nekoliko praznih pridjeva. U vrijeme umjetne inteligencije dodao bih još nešto. Ni brend menadžer ne smije postati moron samo zato što mu je stroj u 58 sekundi napisao nešto što zvuči pametno.

Promocija nije natječaj za najbolju rečenicu. To je upravljanje pažnjom, značenjem i memorijom brenda.

Zato vlasnik tvrtke smije imati ideju. Direktor izvoza smije imati ideju. Prodavač smije imati ideju. I šofer smije imati ideju. Neke od njih čak mogu biti genijalne.

Ali mišljenje nije isto što i odluka. Ako svi upravljaju sloganom, nitko ne upravlja brendom.

U eri AI-ja kreativna prednost više neće biti imati više ideja. Imat će ih svi. Prednost će biti znati koja je vaša.

AI može:

generirati tisuće naziva i slogana, pisati i prepravljati copy, testirati varijante, lokalizirati poruke, personalizirati sadržaj, tražiti neočekivane kombinacije riječi, analizirati reakcije kupaca i neumorno proizvoditi nove mogućnosti.

Brend menadžer mora:

definirati kreativnu strategiju, pronaći ljudska proturječja iza poruke, odlučiti što brend smije reći i što nikada neće reći, razlikovati dobar slogan od slogana koji može živjeti dvadeset godina, zaštititi distinktivnost od algoritamske prosječnosti i – najteže od svega – izbrisati 9.999 sasvim dobrih prijedloga.

Sljedeći članak: AI zna tko izgleda kao brend menadžer. Ne zna tko će znati voditi brend →

In previous articles, we said the 4Ps aren't four departments, but four versions of the same promise. Product says what we offer. Price says how much we think it's worth. Place says where we expect the customer to find us. Promotion says why anyone, amid all that market noise, should bother to look up and pay attention.

And that's exactly why Promotion should be the natural property of marketing and of the brand manager who runs the whole marketing mix.

Not because marketing has a monopoly on cleverness. But because someone has to have a monopoly on accountability.

And yet there's no area of marketing where I've heard more amateurish nonsense over my career than promotion, especially when it comes to the logo, colour, packaging, name and tagline. From owners, export directors, people in sales and accounting, to the key account's three-year-old daughter, all the way to the boss's driver — everyone feels a little bit like a creative director.

Promotion seems to be the one piece of marketing property that everyone else has the right to walk into without knocking.

Until yesterday, that creative process still had a natural limit. Someone would say: "My logo's too small." Another would add: "I don't like the colour." A third would note: "Why don't we add another word to explain it better?" Someone would mutter under their breath: "Could the product be a bit more premium?" Then the one who'd missed the meeting would show up and say: "I'm missing something that speaks to tradition." Or: "Why is the packaging so empty?" The director would say: "Why don't we just write that we're the best?" and add: "My daughter says this would look better in black." The owner's son-in-law would chime in: "The owner likes what the competition is doing." Once everything was finally locked down, sales would revolt: "The customer suggested we add a heart." "Why isn't the name understandable in every market?" "This sounds too marketing-y to me." "This doesn't sound marketing-y at all to me." "Let's do one more version, just so we have a choice."

Not anymore.

Today, while waiting for his boss, the driver will open ChatGPT out of boredom, paste in the marketing proposal, and type: "Give me ten better taglines." Within thirty seconds he'll be reporting to his boss: "Here. AI says it can do better." Or: "See, I told you those marketing people don't do anything."

Oh dear.

The problem is, he'll often be right.

AI is, apparently, damn good at branding. At least the part of it that comes down to words and visuals

One 2025 study of advertising taglines touched our creative ego in a particular way. Taglines written by ChatGPT were rated higher quality — fresher, more meaningful, more persuasive — than human-written ones. But they were less diverse. AI was especially helpful for people who were otherwise less creative to begin with (Chat-GPT4 Does Enhance Creativity. But Human Ego Can Hamper its Potential).

Translated into marketing language: AI won't turn every driver into David Ogilvy. But it will dramatically raise their creative average.

That changes the balance of power. Because in the past, a bad name or tagline still required at least some confidence and a senior enough title. Today all you need is a prompt.

Which is why AI's biggest danger in promotion is exactly its biggest advantage: it can produce an endless supply of good-enough solutions.

Ten taglines. A hundred. A thousand. Ten thousand.

A marketing amateur, by contrast, usually has a good idea arrive on its own. Often in an unusual place. And on an even more unusual occasion.

In the bathroom. On the motorway. At a team-building event. From a director who dreamed up a tagline. From his nephew who "does something with TikTok." From a salesperson who mispronounced the product name. From a distributor who said: "Nobody here will understand that." Or when someone simply wants to repay a favour to a long-time ice-cream customer.

A marketing cynic might say that a creative output is, after all, a kind of controlled hallucination. And if AI is a fish in water at anything, it's hallucinating.

Take brand names. Brand names were being created as today's naming prompt long before ChatGPT. For example, George Eastman invented the word Kodak. It didn't mean photography. It didn't mean camera. It wasn't the name of a city, a daughter, a mistress, or a Greek god of light. It meant — nothing. Eastman simply liked the letter K because it sounded strong and sharp to him, so, as he later explained, he tried a large number of combinations of letters starting and ending with K. And that's how Kodak was born.

Today, AI would finish that job before Eastman managed to sharpen his pencil: "Give me 1,000 short, five-letter names that start and end with the letter K, sound strong, have no existing meaning, and are easy to pronounce in different languages." Enter.

Kodak might be among them. And maybe another 37 better ones too.

But AI would only solve the easier part of the problem: it would produce names.

Eastman did the harder part. He had a criterion for why one of them should be chosen.

That's the distinction we'll have to keep repeating throughout the AI era:

A machine can invent a name. The brand manager has to know why that particular name will mean something to people.

A tagline isn't a sentence. A tagline is compressed strategy.

Nike's Just Do It wasn't created because Dan Wieden asked a computer for "ten motivational taglines for athletic footwear." By his own account, he found one of his inspirations in a rather unlikely place: the last words of American criminal Gary Gilmore before his execution — "Let's do it." Wieden reshaped that association into the three words Nike has carried for decades. Even Phil Knight initially thought they didn't need a tagline like that.

Picture that tagline today.

AI would, in the meantime, churn out another 100 alternatives: Move Your Limits. Own Your Potential. Become Your Best. Push Beyond. Find Your Power ...

All correct. Professional. And all dead on arrival.

Which brings us to the key question behind this article.

If everyone can now produce a good tagline, who decides which one is the right one?

AI is a divergent machine. It will open up countless possibilities for you to consider. Give it a brief — "We need a tagline for a premium toothpaste" — and you'll get 10 suggestions. Ask for more, and it'll give you a hundred. It'll change the tone, rhythm, metaphor, target audience, length and style. It never gets tired, never falls in love with its own idea, and has no problem discarding 100 proposals in five minutes and making 100 new ones.

Marketing's problem, however, is very rarely a lack of ideas.

The problem is that someone has to decide which of them deserves to become the brand.

The brand manager has to say: this tagline sounds good, but it isn't ours. This one is fun, but it doesn't support the positioning. This one will get clicks, but will sound stupid in three years. This one may be less attractive on first read, but has the potential to become part of the brand's language. That's why we say the brand manager has to be a convergent authority.

And the more options AI produces, the more valuable that authority becomes.

When AI should be let off the chain

Let's not, out of fear for our own jobs, banish AI to the basement next to the printer.

In certain parts of copywriting, the machine is already brutal.

JPMorgan Chase started experimenting back in 2016 with Persado, an AI platform that generates and tests marketing language — from headlines and CTAs down to individual words and phrasing. In testing, some AI-generated ad versions achieved up to a 450% higher click-through rate than comparable ads. A particularly interesting observation from then-CMO Kristin Lemkau: AI wrote headlines and copy that a marketer, relying on experience and subjective judgment, probably would never have dared to write.

That's exactly where I want AI.

Let it produce 100 email subject lines. Let it localise copy for 37 markets. Let it make hundreds of CTAs. Let it analyse old campaigns. Let it hunt for clichés. Let it find the sentence I didn't see.

But let it never, ever decide what my brand means.

AI without a brand strategy is just an intern who knows how to post on TikTok.

Virgin Voyages, Branson's cruise company, built an AI version of Jennifer Lopez — Jen AI. The idea was for an AI avatar of Jennifer Lopez to personally invite your friends or family on a trip together.

Where did that idea come from? Try gathering six friends in their fifties to go on holiday together. One has no money. Another has no time. A third has to ask his wife. A fourth says "let's talk." A fifth doesn't answer WhatsApp. And the sixth one is you.

Generative AI let users create a personalised video invitation in which a digital J.Lo invites those six people on a cruise together, tailoring the message to whatever occasion they're celebrating. That made possible a scale of personalisation that one actual Jennifer Lopez, luckily for her, could never deliver in person. According to the company's own figures, more than 25,000 personalised invitations were created, nearly 200,000 people used the microsite, and the campaign achieved enormous global reach. But the real creative idea wasn't:

"Let's make an AI version of Jennifer Lopez."

Any intern could suggest that after two hours of browsing TikTok ads.

The real idea was:

If the biggest problem with a trip together is getting people to say yes, let Jennifer Lopez invite them instead of you.

That's where technology stops being the idea. It becomes the execution of the idea.

AI turned one famous person into tens of thousands of personal invitations. But a human still had to understand why anyone would want to send one in the first place.

That's the difference between an AI trick and marketing. AI was an excellent servant here because it was given a good assignment.

Kantar's recent analysis of AI advertising shows just how important that difference is. The authors analysed hundreds of GenAI ads, combining survey research with facial coding — measuring spontaneous emotional reactions. The results showed that more than 40% of ads in which GenAI was tailored to the brand's tone of voice and distinctive assets ranked among the ads with the strongest brand recognition, while ads with unnatural, distracting visuals of the kind AI tends to produce scored weaker branding on average. Kantar's conclusion is actually quite simple: AI doesn't have your brand "in its heart" unless you've built it in beforehand ("Rethinking AI-generated advertising: how real people really react").

Sometimes the most important creative decision is — NO.

Marketing's true ownership of promotion isn't best seen when marketing says what we will do. It's seen when it says what we won't.

Dove extended its Real Beauty principle into the AI era and explicitly committed to never using AI-generated images in place of real women, nor digitally manufacturing unattainable beauty ideals. The technology can. The brand decided it mustn't. That's brand management.

Not the question:

Can we make this?

But rather:

Is this ours?

Ogilvy warned that the consumer isn't a moron and that you won't convince them with a tagline and a handful of empty adjectives. In the age of artificial intelligence, I'd add one more thing. The brand manager mustn't become a moron either, just because a machine wrote something that sounds clever in 58 seconds.

Promotion isn't a competition for the best sentence. It's the management of attention, meaning, and brand memory.

So the company owner is allowed to have an idea. The export director is allowed to have an idea. The salesperson is allowed to have an idea. And the driver is allowed to have an idea. Some of them might even be brilliant.

But an opinion isn't the same as a decision. If everyone runs the tagline, nobody runs the brand.

In the AI era, the creative advantage will no longer be having more ideas. Everyone will have them. The advantage will be knowing which one is yours.

AI can:

generate thousands of names and taglines, write and rewrite copy, test variants, localise messages, personalise content, hunt for unexpected word combinations, analyse customer reactions, and tirelessly produce new possibilities.

The brand manager must:

define the creative strategy, find the human contradiction behind the message, decide what the brand may say and what it will never say, tell a good tagline apart from one that can live for twenty years, protect distinctiveness from algorithmic averageness, and — hardest of all — delete 9,999 perfectly good suggestions.
