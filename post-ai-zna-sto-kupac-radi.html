AI zna što kupac radi. Ne zna što ga pokreće

AI Knows What the Customer Does. It Doesn't Know What Drives Them

Podatak govori što se dogodilo. Insight pokušava objasniti zašto se dogodilo, koje proturječje otkriva i što bi brend zbog toga trebao učiniti.

A data point tells you what happened. An insight tries to explain why it happened, what contradiction it reveals, and what the brand should do about it.

Branimir Zoraja 16. kolovoza 2026.August 16, 2026 8 min čitanja8 min read

AI danas može analizirati milijune kupnji, recenzija, komentara, pretraga i razgovora s kupcima. Može otkriti da kupci koji u srijedu kupe pastu za zube u petak češće naručuju sredstvo za uklanjanje mrlja. Može ustanoviti da ljudi dulje gledaju plavu ambalažu, ali češće kupuju crvenu. Može pronaći riječ koja se u negativnim recenzijama pojavljuje 37 % češće od ostalih.

Impresivno? Da. Insight? Ne nužno.

Podatak govori što se dogodilo. Insight pokušava objasniti zašto se dogodilo, koje proturječje otkriva i što bi brend zbog toga trebao učiniti.

AI može sažeti tisuću recenzija proizvoda i zaključiti da kupci žele praktičnost, kvalitetu i dobru cijenu. Problem je što to nije potrošački uvid. To je otprilike kao otkriti da ljudi žele biti zdravi, sretni i voljeni.

Parafrazirajući Kahnemana, mogli bismo reći: brzi dio čovjeka kupi, a spori dio nakon kupnje sastavi pristojno obrazloženje.

Kupac nije samo nepouzdan svjedok vlastita ponašanja. On je često i njegov naknadni glasnogovornik. AI može analizirati glasnogovornika. Marketing mora razumjeti čovjeka.

Podaci nisu ljudi

Kupci za sobom ostavljaju tragove: račune, klikove, lokacije, recenzije, povrate, kartice vjernosti i nedovršene košarice. Ako mi pošaljete jedan račun iz dm-a, mogao bih na temelju samo tog računa pronaći do 16 informacija o vama, a ako mi mjesec dana svaki tjedan šaljete račune o kupnji u dm-u, možda i do 33.

Ali trag nije cijeli čovjek.

Na primjer, AI može vidjeti da je kupac odustao od kupnje na online shopu u 21:58. Ne vidi nužno da mu je u tom trenutku zazvonio telefon, zaplakalo dijete, istekla baterija ili da se jednostavno predomislio jer je proizvod na fotografiji izgledao kao nešto što bi pristojno primio na poklon, a nikad ne bi sam kupio.

Digitalni trag pokazuje što je kupac napravio. Ne otkriva uvijek što ga je na to navelo.

Air care brend Febreze je dobar primjer. P&G je prvotno razvio brend sredstava za uklanjanje neugodnih mirisa s tkanina. Logika je bila besprijekorna: ljudi imaju neugodne mirise, proizvod ih uklanja, ljudi će ga kupovati. Samo su previdjeli da se ljudi na stalne mirise u vlastitom domu navikavaju i često ih više ne registriraju. Problem je bio stvaran, ali za kupca nevidljiv.

Tek je promatranje ljudi tijekom čišćenja otkrilo drukčiju ulogu proizvoda. Pozicionirali su Febreze kao mirisnu završnicu rituala čišćenja doma s izraženijim mirisima. Miris u proizvodu nije bio samo uklanjanje problema. Brend je postao nagrada na kraju posla, osjetilni dokaz da je prostor čist. Ali to nije bila samo bolja kampanja. Promijenio se sustav proizvoda, uporabe, navike i komunikacije.

Parafrazirajući Druckera, mogli bismo reći: vrijednost se ne nalazi u sastavu proizvoda. Nastaje u životu kupca.

Zato budućnost marketinga ne pripada samo boljim analitičkim podacima, nego prije svega pametnim sustavima koji povezuju ono što je kupac kupio s promatranjem kako bira i koristi proizvod, korisničku službu s razvojem proizvoda, a prodajne rezultate sa stvarnim mjestima uporabe.

Podaci bez terena samo su vrlo uredan način da ne vidite čovjeka.

Sintetički kupac uvijek lijepo odgovara

AI može proizvesti fantastičnu buyer personu korisnice premium paste za zube koja čita sastav zubne paste kao vinsku etiketu i vjeruje da se premium osmijeh može istisnuti iz tube ili urbanog vlasnika psa koji vikendom planinari, a radnim danom naručuje organsku hranu.

Sintetički će potrošač strpljivo odgovoriti na svako pitanje. Razumjet će brief. Sjetit će se pozicioniranja. Govorit će u cjelovitim rečenicama. Nikada neće kasniti na fokusnu skupinu niti će deset minuta objašnjavati gdje je parkirao.

Idealni ispitanik, ako ćemo parafrazirati Maxa Webera.

Nažalost, takav ne postoji.

Nema kupaonicu, kredit, svekrvu, loše jutro, bolesno dijete ni stvarnu policu s 156 pasti za zube pred sobom. Ne mora se pitati vrijedi li vaša pasta koliko dvije druge na akciji. Ne riskira vlastiti novac.

Istraživanja sintetičkih persona pokazuju da AI modeli mogu reproducirati široke obrasce i asocijacije, ali i pojačavati stereotipe, metodološke pristranosti i odstupanja od ponašanja stvarnih populacija. Što odgovor AI-a zvuči uvjerljivije, to marketinški stručnjak lakše zaboravi da uvjerljivost nije isto što i istinitost.

LEGO je problem potrošačke simulacije riješio mnogo prije današnjih sintetičkih fokusnih skupina.

Na platformi LEGO Ideas stvarni ljudi predlažu buduće proizvode. Ideja mora privući 10 000 stvarnih podržavatelja prije nego što uđe u službeni LEGO-ov proces procjene. Ni tada nije automatski prihvaćena: tek slijedi provjera izvedivosti, portfelja, licenci i uklapanja u brend. Deset tisuća klikova još nisu deset tisuća kupnji. Ali to je ipak više od odgovora sintetičkog ljubitelja LEGO igračaka koji nema ni jednu LEGO kocku pod krevetom.

Pouka nije da marketinško istraživanje treba pretvoriti u glasovanje. Pouka je da kvalitetan marketinški sustav od potrošača traži postupno veći dokaz interesa: odgovor, izbor, registraciju, preporuku, predbilježbu, kupnju i ponovljenu kupnju.

AI može proizvesti hipotezu o tome što bi ljudi mogli željeti. Pravi ljudi moraju uložiti nešto svoje kako bismo povjerovali da im je do toga zaista stalo.

Insight nije sažetak. Insight je paradoks

Najvrjedniji potrošački uvidi obično nisu najčešće rečenice u istraživanju.

Najčešće se kriju u proturječjima.

Kupac govori da želi inovativnu pastu za zube, ali kupuje ono što poznaje. Tvrdi da mu cijena nije presudna, ali čeka akciju. Želi jednostavan izbor, ali očekuje personalizaciju. Traži održiv proizvod, ali ne želi mijenjati vlastite navike. Govori da vjeruje stručnjacima, a kupuje prema preporuci prijateljice. Tvrdi da ne vjeruje oglašavanju, ali pamti brendove koje je oglašavanje izgradilo. Kaže da mu ambalaža nije važna, ali proizvod bez dobre ambalaže često ni ne uzme u ruku. Želi da ga brend poznaje, ali ne želi otkriti da ga brend zaista poznaje. Kaže da kupuje proizvod. Često kupuje mir, status, naviku, opravdanje ili pet minuta lakšeg života.

Kupac je kao tvoja žena, rekao bi David Ogilvy: nju možeš prevariti samo prvi put.

AI može pronaći ta proturječja. Marketinški stručnjak mora procijeniti koje je od tih proturječja poslovno važno, može li ga brend vjerodostojno riješiti i je li rješenje samo komunikacijsko ili mora promijeniti cijeli brend sustav.

Patagonia, sportski i outdoor brend, mogla je napraviti još jednu kampanju u kojoj bi objašnjavala koliko je važna održivost njezine odjeće i opreme. Umjesto toga razvila je koncept Worn Wear: sustav popravaka, ponovne uporabe, otkupa i prodaje rabljene odjeće i outdoor opreme. Patagonia je dosad, prema vlastitim podacima, objavila i više od stotinu vodiča koji ljudima pomažu da sami održavaju i popravljaju njezine proizvode.

Tu počinje pravi paradoks: prodati više tako da kupac kupuje rjeđe. Koji će AI algoritam takvo što predložiti?

Nikad!

Kupac želi trošiti odgovornije, ali želi dobru odjeću. Brend želi prodavati, ali želi da proizvod traje. Klasična marketinška logika kaže da tvrtka mora ubrzati zamjenu starog proizvoda novim. Patagonia je dio vrijednosti pronašla upravo u odgađanju te zamjene.

Parafrazirajući Roryja Sutherlanda, rekli bismo da suprotnost dobroj ideji katkad nije loša ideja. Katkad je druga dobra ideja.

Uvid je paradoks, ili, ako hoćete blaže, napetost koju je organizacija spremna riješiti — čak i kada rješenje nije najudobnije za vlastiti poslovni model.

AI proizvodi hipotezu. Tržište donosi presudu

AI, dakle, može povezati podatke o kupcima, koje brend menadžer u najvećem broju slučajeva ne bi stigao ni otvoriti. Može predvidjeti koja će se Patagonijina jakna svidjeti kojem kupcu, koji će kroj vjerojatno vratiti i koja kombinacija boje, cijene i veličine ima najveću vjerojatnost kupnje.

Ali vjerojatnost nije kupnja.

Uzmimo sljedeći primjer. Colgate koristi AI kako bi među tisućama mogućih kombinacija sastojaka predvidio koje formule imaju najbolju ravnotežu učinkovitosti, teksture, okusa, stabilnosti i cijene. Ali algoritam ne pere zube. Formulu još moraju napraviti kemičari, provjeriti stručnjaci i, na kraju, staviti je u usta pravi ljudi. Colgate Max Fresh dobar je primjer posljednjeg dijela tog procesa. Razgovori s korisnicima pokazali su da žele da osjećaj svježine traje dulje, pa su godine formuliranja i testiranja u Colgateu, prema vlastitim podacima, dovele do tehnologije UltraFreeze, kojom su pokušali povećati osjećaj hlađenja tijekom pranja zubi i znatno produljiti osjećaj svježine nakon pranja.

O tome koliko će umjetna inteligencija doista mijenjati inovacije u marketingu možemo nekom drugom prilikom. Za sada AI stvarno može predložiti da će određena kombinacija mentola, arome i aktivnih sastojaka produljiti osjećaj svježine nakon pranja. No samo potrošač nakon pranja odlučuje je li zaista bolja. Je li okus prejak? Pjeni li se dovoljno? Ostaje li osjećaj svježine? Hoće li pastu kupiti ponovno? Svaki odgovor, test i ponovljena kupnja novi su dokaz kojim se početna hipoteza potvrđuje, mijenja ili odbacuje.

Parafrazirajući Poppera, mogli bismo reći: dobra hipoteza mora biti spremna umrijeti kada se susretne s dokazom.

AI procesuira podatak koji stvara hipotezu. Stručnjak mu dodaje kontekst. Ponuda odlazi kupcu. Kupac glasuje novcem i ponovnom narudžbom. Rezultat se vraća u sustav.

U ljudskom marketingu se, ponekad, događa suprotno. Kada tržište odbije našu ideju, promijenimo ciljnu skupinu, razdoblje mjerenja ili boju grafikona. Proizvod nije podbacio. „Tržište još nije sazrelo.“ Kupci ga nisu odbili. „Nisu dovoljno educirani.“

AI nam sad pomaže proizvesti još uvjerljivija opravdanja. Zato će u budućnosti biti važnije izgraditi marketinški sustav koji brzo ubija loše hipoteze nego sustav koji brzo proizvodi nove sadržaje.

Od promocije do sustava

U prethodnom članku pokazali smo da promocija ima početak, kraj i završni izvještaj, a marketinški sustav ima pamćenje.

Promocija govori. Sustav sluša, promatra, ispituje, testira i mijenja se. Promocija može kratkoročno popraviti rezultat. Sustav mora objasniti zašto se rezultat promijenio i prenijeti to znanje u proizvod, cijenu, distribuciju, komunikaciju i sljedeću odluku.

Febreze je promatranje pretvorio u naviku. LEGO je interes pretvorio u zajednicu i razvojni proces. Patagonia je potrošački paradoks pretvorila u uslugu. Colgate je potvrdio da zubna pasta ima kupaonicu i ponovljenu kupnju.

To je budućnost marketinga.

Ne upravljanje većim brojem kampanja, nego upravljanje sustavom koji iz svakog kontakta postaje malo pametniji.

AI će analizirati sve što je kupac rekao. Samo ostaje problem što kupac često ne radi ono što kaže, ne kaže ono što misli i ne zna zašto je kupio. A dok AI to napokon otkrije, možda je već kupio konkurentski proizvod.

AI će možda upravljati sve većim dijelom marketinškog stroja. Ali samo će brend menadžer uvijek znati kamo taj stroj vozi, zašto bi kupac u njega ušao i ima li brend uopće pravo određivati kupcu smjer.

AI može obraditi milijune podataka; otkriti obrazac; sažeti što su potrošači rekli; proizvesti buyer personu, simulirati fokusnu skupinu i predvidjeti vjerojatno ponašanje; stvarati hipoteze brže nego što smo ih ikada mogli napisati i, na kraju, ustanoviti da se nešto prodaje.

Brend menadžer mora odlučiti koji mu podatak nešto znači; prepoznati paradokse ili unakrsne napetosti koje pokreću kupca, ili ono što nije rekao, što je prešutio i zašto je na kraju učinio nešto sasvim drugo; organizirati sustav koji će hipoteze provjeriti, pogrešne odbaciti, a dobre pretvoriti u proizvod, cijenu, distribuciju, uslugu i promociju; i, na kraju, mora razumjeti prodaje li se zbog brenda, akcije, navike, nedostatka boljeg izbora ili zato što je proizvod slučajno bio na visini očiju.

Today, AI can analyse millions of purchases, reviews, comments, searches and customer conversations. It can discover that customers who buy toothpaste on a Wednesday are more likely to order a stain remover on Friday. It can establish that people look longer at blue packaging but buy red more often. It can find the word that appears 37% more often than others in negative reviews.

Impressive? Yes. An insight? Not necessarily.

A data point tells you what happened. An insight tries to explain why it happened, what contradiction it reveals, and what the brand should do about it.

AI can summarise a thousand product reviews and conclude that customers want convenience, quality and a good price. The problem is that this isn't a consumer insight. It's roughly on the level of discovering that people want to be healthy, happy and loved.

To paraphrase Kahneman: the fast part of a person buys, and the slow part composes a respectable justification afterwards.

The customer isn't just an unreliable witness to their own behaviour. They're often its after-the-fact spokesperson too. AI can analyse the spokesperson. Marketing has to understand the human.

Data isn't people

Customers leave a trail behind them: receipts, clicks, locations, reviews, returns, loyalty cards and abandoned carts. If you sent me a single receipt from a drugstore, I could probably find up to 16 pieces of information about you from that receipt alone — and if you sent me a week's drugstore receipt every week for a month, maybe up to 33.

But the trail isn't the whole person.

For instance, AI can see that a customer abandoned a purchase on an online shop at 9:58pm. It doesn't necessarily see that their phone rang at that moment, their child started crying, their battery died, or that they simply changed their mind because the product in the photo looked like something they'd be happy to receive as a gift but would never buy for themselves.

The digital trail shows what the customer did. It doesn't always reveal what drove them to do it.

The air-care brand Febreze is a good example. P&G originally developed it as a brand of products that remove unpleasant odours from fabric. The logic was flawless: people have bad smells, the product removes them, people will buy it. They just overlooked the fact that people grow used to the constant smells in their own home and often stop noticing them at all. The problem was real, but invisible to the customer.

Only by observing people while they cleaned did a different role for the product emerge. They repositioned Febreze as the fragrant finishing touch to a home-cleaning ritual, with more pronounced scents. The scent in the product wasn't just about removing a problem anymore. The brand became the reward at the end of the chore, the sensory proof that the space was clean. But this wasn't just a better campaign. The whole system changed — the product, the usage, the habit, and the communication.

To paraphrase Drucker: value isn't found in the product's formula. It's created in the customer's life.

That's why the future of marketing doesn't just belong to better analytics, but above all to smart systems that connect what the customer bought with observing how they choose and use the product, that connect customer service with product development, and that connect sales results with the real places the product gets used.

Data without fieldwork is just a very tidy way of not seeing the human being.

The synthetic customer always gives a nice answer

AI can produce a fantastic buyer persona: a premium toothpaste user who reads the ingredients list like a wine label and believes a premium smile can be squeezed out of a tube, or an urban dog owner who goes hiking on weekends and orders organic food on weekdays.

The synthetic consumer will patiently answer every question. They'll understand the brief. They'll remember the positioning. They'll speak in complete sentences. They'll never be late to a focus group or spend ten minutes explaining where they parked.

The ideal respondent, to paraphrase Max Weber.

Unfortunately, no such person exists.

They don't have a bathroom, a loan, a mother-in-law, a bad morning, a sick child, or a real shelf with 156 toothpastes standing in front of them. They never have to ask themselves whether your paste is worth as much as two others on promotion. They never risk their own money.

Research into synthetic personas shows that AI models can reproduce broad patterns and associations, but can just as easily amplify stereotypes, methodological biases, and deviations from how real populations actually behave. The more convincing an AI's answer sounds, the easier it is for a marketer to forget that being convincing isn't the same as being true.

LEGO solved the problem of consumer simulation long before today's synthetic focus groups.

On the LEGO Ideas platform, real people propose future products. An idea needs to attract 10,000 real supporters before it even enters LEGO's official review process. Even then it isn't automatically accepted — feasibility, portfolio fit, licensing and brand fit checks follow. Ten thousand clicks still aren't ten thousand purchases. But that's still worth more than the answer of a synthetic LEGO fan who doesn't have a single brick under their bed.

The lesson isn't that marketing research should turn into a vote. The lesson is that a good marketing system asks the consumer for progressively stronger proof of interest: a response, a choice, a sign-up, a referral, a pre-order, a purchase, and a repeat purchase.

AI can produce a hypothesis about what people might want. Real people have to put something of their own on the line before we believe they actually care.

Insight Isn't a Summary. Insight Is a Paradox

The most valuable consumer insights usually aren't the most common sentences in the research.

They usually hide in the contradictions.

The customer says they want an innovative toothpaste, but buys what they already know. They claim price isn't decisive, but wait for the promotion. They want a simple choice, but expect personalisation. They ask for a sustainable product, but don't want to change their own habits. They say they trust experts, but buy on a friend's recommendation. They claim they don't trust advertising, but remember the brands that advertising built. They say packaging doesn't matter to them, but will often never even pick up a product without good packaging. They want the brand to know them, but don't want to discover that the brand really does know them. They say they're buying a product. What they're often buying is peace of mind, status, habit, an excuse, or five minutes of an easier life.

The customer is like your wife, David Ogilvy would say: you can only fool her once.

AI can find those contradictions. The marketer has to judge which of those contradictions actually matters commercially, whether the brand can credibly resolve it, and whether the solution is purely a communications fix or has to change the whole brand system.

Patagonia, the sports and outdoor brand, could have made yet another campaign explaining how important the sustainability of its clothing and gear is. Instead, it built the Worn Wear concept: a system for repairing, reusing, buying back and reselling used clothing and outdoor gear. According to its own data, Patagonia has so far published more than a hundred guides that help people maintain and repair its products themselves.

That's where the real paradox begins: selling more by getting the customer to buy less often. Which AI algorithm is going to propose something like that?

Never!

The customer wants to spend more responsibly, but still wants good clothing. The brand wants to sell, but wants the product to last. Classic marketing logic says a company has to speed up the replacement of the old product with the new. Patagonia found part of its value in delaying that replacement instead.

To paraphrase Rory Sutherland: the opposite of a good idea isn't always a bad idea. Sometimes it's another good idea.

An insight is a paradox, or, put more gently, a tension the organisation is willing to resolve — even when the resolution isn't the most comfortable one for its own business model.

AI produces a hypothesis. The market delivers the verdict

So AI can connect customer data that a brand manager would, in most cases, never even have time to open. It can predict which Patagonia jacket a given customer will like, which cut they're likely to return, and which combination of colour, price and size has the highest probability of being bought.

But probability isn't a purchase.

Take the following example. Colgate uses AI to predict, among thousands of possible ingredient combinations, which formulas have the best balance of efficacy, texture, taste, stability and cost. But the algorithm doesn't brush anyone's teeth. The formula still has to be made by chemists, checked by experts, and, in the end, put in real people's mouths. Colgate Max Fresh is a good example of that final part of the process. Conversations with users showed they wanted the feeling of freshness to last longer, so, according to its own data, years of formulation and testing at Colgate led to UltraFreeze technology, which tried to increase the cooling sensation while brushing and significantly extend the feeling of freshness afterwards.

How much artificial intelligence will truly change innovation in marketing is a conversation for another time. For now, AI really can propose that a certain combination of menthol, flavour and active ingredients will extend the feeling of freshness after brushing. But only the consumer, after brushing, decides whether it's actually better. Is the taste too strong? Does it foam enough? Does the fresh feeling last? Will they buy the paste again? Every answer, every test, and every repeat purchase is new evidence that confirms, revises, or discards the original hypothesis.

To paraphrase Popper: a good hypothesis has to be willing to die when it meets the evidence.

AI processes the data that generates the hypothesis. The expert adds context to it. The offer goes out to the customer. The customer votes with their money and their next order. The result feeds back into the system.

In human marketing, sometimes the opposite happens. When the market rejects our idea, we change the target group, the measurement period, or the colour of the chart. The product didn't fail. "The market just isn't ready yet." Customers didn't reject it. "They're just not educated enough."

AI now helps us produce even more convincing excuses. That's why, in the future, it will matter more to build a marketing system that kills bad hypotheses quickly than one that produces new content quickly.

From promotion to system

In the previous article, we showed that promotion has a start, an end and a final report, while a marketing system has memory.

Promotion talks. A system listens, observes, questions, tests and changes. Promotion can fix a result in the short term. A system has to explain why the result changed, and carry that knowledge into the product, the price, the distribution, the communication, and the next decision.

Febreze turned observation into a habit. LEGO turned interest into a community and a development process. Patagonia turned a consumer paradox into a service. Colgate confirmed that toothpaste has a bathroom and a repeat purchase.

That's the future of marketing.

Not managing a greater number of campaigns, but managing a system that gets a little smarter from every interaction.

AI will analyse everything the customer said. The only problem that remains is that the customer often doesn't do what they say, doesn't say what they think, and doesn't know why they bought what they bought. And by the time AI finally figures it out, they may have already bought a competitor's product.

AI may end up running an ever larger part of the marketing machine. But only the brand manager will always know where that machine is driving to, why the customer would want to get in, and whether the brand even has the right to decide the customer's direction.

AI can process millions of data points; detect a pattern; summarise what consumers said; produce a buyer persona, simulate a focus group, and predict likely behaviour; generate hypotheses faster than we could ever write them; and, in the end, establish that something is selling.

The brand manager has to decide which data point actually means something; recognise the paradoxes or cross-cutting tensions that drive the customer, or what they didn't say, what they left unspoken, and why they ended up doing something completely different; organise a system that tests hypotheses, discards the wrong ones, and turns the good ones into product, price, distribution, service and promotion; and, in the end, has to understand whether something is selling because of the brand, a promotion, a habit, a lack of a better option, or simply because the product happened to be at eye level on the shelf.
