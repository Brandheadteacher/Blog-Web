Svijet reklame — AI & marketing consulting

AI zna tko izgleda kao brend menadžer. Ne zna tko će znati voditi brend

AI Knows Who Looks Like a Brand Manager. It Doesn't Know Who Will Know How to Run a Brand

AI može pronaći kandidata koji odgovara oglasu. Ne može pronaći brend menadžera koji odgovara brendu.

AI can find a candidate who matches the job ad. It can't find a brand manager who matches the brand.

Branimir Zoraja 6. rujna 2026.September 6, 2026 7 min čitanja7 min read

Uvijek lajkam neoliberalne, bihevioralne anegdotice recruitera o zapošljavanju u marketingu na LinkedInu. Dijelom zato što smatram da LinkedIn i jest medij i glasnogovornik neoliberalne paradigme pa kad sam tamo trebam se ponašati prema njihovim pravilima, a dijelom zato što stvarno želim poduprijeti ljude koji pišu o marketingu, pa makar bilo kako. Ne postoji loš publicitet, rekao bi P. T. Burnum. A očajno je malo ljudi iz moje poslovne okoline koji su u trideset godina napisali bilo što dulje od maila „FYI, pls feedback ASAP".

Posebno su mi dragi nudge savjeti: ako nemaš posao, popravi CV; ako te ne primjećuju, pojačaj signal. Ako ne prođeš intervju, poradi na storytellingu. Ako stagniraš, radi na personal brandingu; ako si izgorio, nauči postavljati granice. Ako ti je šef idiot, razvij resilience. Gledaj iz druge perspektive, diferenciraj se, prestani govoriti što si radio i počni govoriti kakav si impact napravio, smanji rizik za poslodavca i budi culture fit i već ćeš sljedeći utorak dobiti tri ponude za posao.

Ma nemoj!

Ali brend menadžer nije pasta za zube, nije semafor, pisac ni fitness trener. A hiring menadžer nije polica u Konzumu.

Sad se još pojavio AI.

Brend menadžer kao proizvod s lošim pakiranjem

Čini se da su od Signal Theory Michaela Spencea iz 70-ih godina recruiteri na LinkedInu malo toga naučili. Spenceova glavna teza je bila da poslodavac ne poznaje stvarnu kvalitetu zaposlenika pa pokušava zaključivati iz signala: obrazovanja, iskustva, rezultata, titule, preporuka.

Drugim riječima, u današnjoj LinkedInovoj verziji kandidat za posao brend menadžera postaje nešto poput proizvoda. Mora biti jasno pozicioniran. Lako čitljiv. Emocionalno uvjerljiv. Diferenciran. Mora imati dokaz. Tri brojke. Jasan benefit. USP, reason to believe.

To je logika u kojoj kompetencija nije dovoljna ako tržište ne može dovoljno brzo prepoznati njezinu vrijednost. Brend menadžer pretvara se u profesionalni brend koji mora optimizirati percepciju.

Fuj!

Da, možda se recruiteru ne da čitati CV dulje od 30 sekundi, kad ih dođe 150, ali nikad neće spomenuti na LinkedInu da je onih 8 koje mu je označio član uprave kao svoje favorite pročitao 15 puta ne bi li im ipak našao neku poveznicu za posao.

Neoliberalni sustav kaže: „Uvijek si ti odgovoran za ishod.“ A zapravo je možda leadership loš. Možda je organizacijska kultura toksična. Možda je menadžer nesposoban. Možda je sustav nagrađivanja besmislen. Možda je posao loše dizajniran. Možda je plaća premala. Možda su kriteriji selekcije glupi.

Drugim riječima: analizira „tko si“, „kako se pozicioniraš i optimiziraš“ i „jesi li konkurentan“, ali manje analizira kvalitetu iskustva rada, timsku inteligenciju, autonomiju i kreativnost – upravo ono od čega dobar brend menadžer živi. To je već politička ekonomija. A ona gadi dobar LinkedIn post.

Kod selekcije ljudi za marketing događa isto što i kod razvoja proizvoda: trial nije repeat

Možete fantastično proći intervju i biti loš brend menadžer. Kao što možete napraviti odličan advertising awareness i loš proizvod.

Možete biti karizmatični, strukturirano govoriti STAR metodom, imati savršeno pripremljene „tri brojke“, CEO-ready prezentaciju i LinkedIn profil na kojem ste istodobno passionate, purpose-driven i humbled to announce. I još uvijek ne znati voditi brend.

Stručnost je nezgodna. Treba vremena da je vidite.

A sada smo uveli AI da nudge teoriju učini još efikasnijom.

AI zna kako napisati oglas. Ne zna koga zapravo tražite

AI može napisati odličan job description za brend menadžera. Može ga učiniti jasnijim, inkluzivnijim, SEO-optimiziranim i „privlačnijim talentu“.

Ali ne zna sam odrediti što je dobar brend menadžer.

Tražimo li nekoga tko će upravljati brendom ili samo njegovim kalendarom? P&L menadžera koji razumije cijenu, maržu i profitabilnost? Čuvara brand equityja? Inovatora? Consumer insight stručnjaka? Nekoga tko povezuje marketing, prodaju, razvoj i financije ili operativca koji će lansiranje dovesti na policu na vrijeme? Nekoga tko koordinira agencije ili budućeg general managera?

Ako je definicija pogrešna, AI će samo napisati elegantniju pogrešku.

Traži se brend menadžer. Ljudi se ne moraju javljati.

LinkedIn već koristi AI za pisanje oglasa za zapošljavanje, traženje i rangiranje kandidata, personaliziranu komunikaciju, prescreening i pregled prijava.

S druge strane kandidat koristi ChatGPT ili neki drugi alat da pročita oglas, optimizira CV, pronađe ključne riječi, napiše motivacijsko pismo i pripremi odgovore za intervju.

Dakle:

AI napiše oglas za brend menadžera. Onda pomogne kandidatu da se prilagodi oglasu. Drugi AI zatim procjenjuje koliko se kandidat dobro prilagodio oglasu koji je možda napisao prvi AI. Ako smo učinkoviti, čovjek se prvi put pojavi tek na razgovoru.

Ili nakon njega.

Ako dovoljno automatiziramo proces, čovjek će nam uskoro samo smetati. A proces zapošljavanja pretvorili smo u vrlo učinkovit razgovor dvaju strojeva.

Kad svi kandidati koriste AI, „signal“ postaje sve manje signal

Ako tržište bira najčitljivijeg, kako nas uvjeravaju na LinkedInu, AI kandidatu za posao u marketingu omogućuje da postane izuzetno čitljiv.

IBM danas čak eksplicitno dopušta kandidatima da koriste AI za poboljšanje gramatike, jasnoće i formatiranja CV-a, sažimanje postignuća te pripremu za intervju (IBM Careers, „Application Steps & Tips – Acceptable Use of AI by Candidates During Recruitment“).

Dakle, AI pomaže kandidatu proizvoditi signal, dok AI na drugoj strani pokušava interpretirati signal.

Kad svi CV-i postanu savršeno čitljivi, čitljivost će prestati biti diferencijator.

AI ne zapošljava prema istini. Zapošljava prema kriteriju. A najveći rizik nije da će AI pogriješiti, nego da će vrlo učinkovito, brzo i dosljedno primjenjivati pogrešnu definiciju dobrog brend menadžera.

Nije ništa neobično kad se recruiteri hvale na LinkedInu da im se ne da čitati detaljan CV. Herbert Simon je još 1971. upozorio da obilje informacija stvara siromaštvo pažnje. („Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World“).

AI taj problem rješava filtriranjem. Ali tu nastaje novo pitanje:

Po kojem kriteriju filtrira? Po kriteriju tri brojke koje ste napisali prema preporuci recruitera?

Ako model učimo na profilima dosadašnjih uspješnih brend menadžera, lako ćemo budućeg talenta pretvoriti u digitalnu kopiju jučerašnjeg dobrog brend menadžera.

Ako mu kažemo da su naši najbolji dosadašnji brend menadžeri završili određeni fakultet, radili u tri poznate kompanije, koristili isti profesionalni vokabular i imali sličan career path, AI može postati izuzetno sofisticiran stroj za zapošljavanje naše prošlosti.

A dobar brend menadžer često mora učiniti upravo suprotno: osporiti category convention, odbaciti „best practice“, ubiti popularnu inovaciju, posvađati se s istraživanjem, braniti identitet brenda kad trend traži da postane netko drugi, reći ne profitabilnoj ideji koja prelazi moralnu granicu, ostati autentičan kad optimizacija savjetuje imitaciju, braniti vrijednosti baš onda kada ih je najskuplje braniti, izabrati kreativnu ideju koju nijedan model ne bi rangirao prvom i vjerovati intuiciji kad potrošač radi nešto što nitko nije predvidio u strategiji.

Unilever je izabrao drukčiji put. Kompanija je godinama među najpoznatijim primjerima digitalizirane i AI-potpomognute selekcije u marketingu, ali njihov današnji opis procesa naglašava upravo kombinaciju tehnologije i ljudskog kontakta. Velik dio bihevioralne tehnologije u zapošljavanju pokušava iz ponašanja zaključiti osobnost. Unileverov proces, barem prema vlastitim podacima, tehnologiju više koristi da bi strukturirao selekciju nego da bi iz digitalnih tragova konstruirao kandidata.

Drugim riječima, AI ne mora glumiti psihologa da bi bio koristan.

Može ukloniti administraciju i recruitere koji nemaju vremena 30 sekundi pročitati nečiju molbu za posao. Pronaći profile. Usporediti iskustva. Upozoriti na rupe u CV-u koje čovjek nije primijetio. Pripremiti pitanja. Može standardizirati dio procjene. Ali nakon toga netko ipak mora razgovarati s kandidatom, postaviti pitanje koje nije bilo u scenariju, čuti odgovor koji nije savršeno strukturiran i procijeniti nešto što se ne može svesti na duljinu pogleda, broj pauza ili način na koji se kandidat nasmiješio u prvih sedam sekundi.

To je važna razlika. A to je možda i zreliji oblik primjene AI-a.

Onboarding počinje nakon što prestanemo prodavati jedni drugima percepciju

Ista stvar vrijedi i nakon odabira kandidata. Prema mojem iskustvu, većina problema s kvalitetom izbora ljudi u marketingu rijetko je bila zbog CV-a napisanog na 7 stranica ili čak cijelog procesa do odluke koga zaposliti. Mnogo češće počinje tek nakon toga — kad izaberemo kandidata, a onda ga prepustimo lošem onboardingu.

AI može složiti raspored onboardinga, objasniti procedure i odgovoriti na 150 FAQ-ova nakon 58. PowerPoint prezentacije koju je novi brend menadžer uredno pročitao, a koju nitko drugi nije otvorio od prošlog godišnjeg sastanka. Može personalizirati edukaciju, podsjećati, mjeriti napredak i paziti da nitko ne preskoči modul broj 17.

Ali brend menadžer mora upoznati memoriju brenda.

Zašto je neka ekstenzija propala. Zašto distributer mrzi određeno pakiranje. Zašto prodaja ne vjeruje novom projektu. Zašto je proizvod koji izgleda uspješno zapravo strateški problem. Zašto je kampanja koju svi danas smatraju genijalnom na početku skoro bila ukinuta. I zašto se određena pogreška u toj kampanji napravila već četvrti put.

To prenose ljudi. Organizacija mora pokazati da je dovoljno dobra da ljudi žele ostati u njoj. Neke tvrtke, zato, uz formalni onboarding novom zaposleniku dodjeljuju buddyja koji mu pomaže razumjeti organizaciju i kulturu.

Što ako problem nije u kandidatu?

Ne mislim, dakle, da trebamo prestati učiti brend menadžere kako se predstavljati. Dobar CV je bolji od lošeg. Jasan odgovor je bolji od mumljanja. A dokaz je bolji od pridjeva.

Ali percepciju ima svatko. Problem počinje kad dojam stručnosti postane dokaz stručnosti.

Ako je kvalitetna percepcija dokaz kvalitetnog izbora, onda bi takav sustav trebao proizvoditi nevjerojatno dobre rezultate.

Ali tvrtke bi trebale prestati tražiti od kandidata da kompenziraju njihove loše kriterije selekcije. Organizacija se mora prilagoditi novom zaposleniku. I stvoriti uvjete da ljudi mogu dugoročno napredovati, biti autentični i održivi.

AI može pronaći kandidata koji savršeno odgovara vašoj percepciji dobrog brend menadžera.

Pravo pitanje je puno neugodnije:

Što ako je problem upravo u vašoj percepciji?

Najlakše je popraviti CV. Mnogo je neugodnije pitati je li problem u sustavu koji ga čita.

Bihevioralni pristup pita: Kako kandidat može poslati bolji signal i percepciju? Politička ekonomija pita: Tko određuje što je dobar signal? Tko ima moć birati? Kako je dizajniran posao? Kolika je plaća? Koliko ljudi odlazi? Zašto odlaze? Tko snosi posljedice lošeg izbora?

W. Edwards Deming rekao je to mnogo jednostavnije: „A bad system will beat a good person every time.“

To bi svaki recruiter trebao zalijepiti pokraj monitora.

I svaki direktor iznad njega.

Brend menadžera zato ne treba birati kao brend. Brend menadžer mora imati stručnost. Mora razumjeti kupca, P&L, proizvod, cijenu, distribuciju i komunikaciju. Mora razlikovati insight od podatka, inovaciju od noviteta i rast brenda od kratkoročnog volumena. Mora znati surađivati. Ali mora ponekad i znati reći ne. Osobito vlastitom šefu.

AI može pronaći brend menadžera koji savršeno odgovara vašem LinkedIn oglasu.

Brend menadžer kojeg stvarno trebate možda je upravo onaj koji će vam prvog dana objasniti što s tim oglasom nije bilo u redu.

Što može AI, a što mora brend menadžer:

AI može napisati oglas za posao. Brend menadžer mora definirati posao. AI može obraditi prijave. Brend menadžer mora definirati kriterij. AI može pronaći uzorke. Brend menadžer mora prepoznati iznimku. AI može ubrzati onboarding. Brend menadžer mora prenijeti memoriju brenda.

Sljedeći članak: AI nema karakter. Brend bi ga morao imati. Tko će mu objasniti razliku? →

I always like the neoliberal, behavioural little anecdotes recruiters post about hiring in marketing on LinkedIn. Partly because I think LinkedIn really is the medium and mouthpiece of the neoliberal paradigm, so when I'm there I feel I should play by its rules, and partly because I genuinely want to support people who write about marketing, however they do it. There's no such thing as bad publicity, P.T. Barnum would say. And there are desperately few people in my professional circle who, in thirty years, have written anything longer than an "FYI, pls feedback ASAP" email.

I'm especially fond of the nudge-style advice: if you don't have a job, fix your CV; if nobody notices you, boost your signal. If you fail the interview, work on your storytelling. If you're stagnating, work on your personal branding; if you're burnt out, learn to set boundaries. If your boss is an idiot, build resilience. Look at it from another angle, differentiate yourself, stop talking about what you did and start talking about the impact you made, reduce the employer's risk, and be a culture fit — and by next Tuesday you'll have three job offers.

Oh, come on!

But a brand manager isn't toothpaste, isn't a traffic light, isn't a writer or a personal trainer. And a hiring manager isn't a shelf at the supermarket.

Now AI has shown up too.

The brand manager as a product with bad packaging

It seems LinkedIn recruiters have learned very little from Michael Spence's Signalling Theory from the 1970s. Spence's main thesis was that the employer doesn't actually know a worker's true quality, so they try to infer it from signals: education, experience, results, titles, references.

In other words, in today's LinkedIn version, a candidate for a brand manager job becomes something like a product. They have to be clearly positioned. Easy to read. Emotionally convincing. Differentiated. They need proof. Three numbers. A clear benefit. A USP, a reason to believe.

It's a logic in which competence isn't enough if the market can't recognise its value quickly enough. The brand manager turns into a professional brand that has to optimise perception.

Ugh.

Yes, a recruiter may not feel like reading a CV for longer than 30 seconds when 150 of them come in, but they'll never mention on LinkedIn that they read the 8 a board member flagged as their favourites fifteen times over, trying to find them some connection to the job anyway.

The neoliberal system says: "You're always responsible for the outcome." But maybe the leadership is actually bad. Maybe the organisational culture is toxic. Maybe the manager is incompetent. Maybe the reward system makes no sense. Maybe the job is badly designed. Maybe the pay is too low. Maybe the selection criteria are stupid.

In other words, it analyses "who you are," "how you position and optimise yourself," and "whether you're competitive," but it analyses far less the quality of someone's work experience, team intelligence, autonomy and creativity — exactly what a good brand manager lives on. That's already political economy. And political economy makes for a lousy LinkedIn post.

The same thing happens when selecting marketing people as when developing products: trial isn't repeat

You can ace an interview and still be a bad brand manager. Just as you can create brilliant advertising awareness and still have a bad product.

You can be charismatic, speak in a structured way using the STAR method, have a perfectly prepared set of "three numbers," a CEO-ready presentation, and a LinkedIn profile where you're simultaneously passionate, purpose-driven, and humbled to announce. And still not know how to run a brand.

Expertise is inconvenient. It takes time to see it.

And now we've brought in AI to make nudge theory even more efficient.

AI knows how to write a job ad. It doesn't know who you're actually looking for

AI can write an excellent job description for a brand manager. It can make it clearer, more inclusive, SEO-optimised, and "more attractive to talent."

But it can't determine on its own what makes a good brand manager.

Are we looking for someone who will manage the brand, or just its calendar? A P&L manager who understands price, margin and profitability? A guardian of brand equity? An innovator? A consumer insight specialist? Someone who connects marketing, sales, R&D and finance, or an operator who'll get the launch onto the shelf on time? Someone who coordinates agencies, or a future general manager?

If the definition is wrong, AI will just write a more elegant mistake.

Brand manager wanted. Humans need not apply.

LinkedIn already uses AI to write job ads, search for and rank candidates, personalise communication, pre-screen, and review applications.

On the other side, the candidate uses ChatGPT or some other tool to read the ad, optimise their CV, find keywords, write a cover letter, and prepare answers for the interview.

So:

AI writes the brand manager ad. Then it helps the candidate adapt to the ad. A second AI then evaluates how well the candidate adapted to an ad that the first AI may have written. If we're efficient, the human only shows up for the first time at the interview.

Or after it.

If we automate the process enough, humans will soon just get in the way. And we'll have turned the hiring process into a very efficient conversation between two machines.

When every candidate uses AI, the "signal" becomes less and less of a signal

If the market picks the most "readable" candidate, as we're assured on LinkedIn, AI lets a marketing job candidate become extremely readable.

IBM today even explicitly allows candidates to use AI to improve the grammar, clarity and formatting of their CV, summarise achievements, and prepare for interviews (IBM Careers, "Application Steps & Tips – Acceptable Use of AI by Candidates During Recruitment").

So AI helps the candidate produce the signal, while AI on the other side tries to interpret the signal.

Once every CV becomes perfectly readable, readability will stop being a differentiator.

AI doesn't hire based on truth. It hires based on criteria. And the biggest risk isn't that AI will get it wrong — it's that it will very efficiently, quickly and consistently apply the wrong definition of a good brand manager.

It's nothing unusual when recruiters brag on LinkedIn that they can't be bothered reading a detailed CV. Herbert Simon warned back in 1971 that an abundance of information creates a poverty of attention ("Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World").

AI solves that problem through filtering. But that raises a new question:

Filtering by what criterion? By the criterion of the three numbers you wrote on the recruiter's advice?

If we train the model on the profiles of today's successful brand managers, we'll easily turn future talent into a digital copy of yesterday's good brand manager.

If we tell it that our best brand managers so far graduated from a particular university, worked at three well-known companies, used the same professional vocabulary, and had a similar career path, AI can become an extremely sophisticated machine for hiring our own past.

And a good brand manager often has to do exactly the opposite: challenge category convention, reject "best practice," kill a popular innovation, pick a fight with the research, defend the brand's identity when the trend demands it become someone else, say no to a profitable idea that crosses a moral line, stay authentic when optimisation advises imitation, defend values precisely when defending them is most expensive, choose the creative idea no model would ever rank first, and trust intuition when the consumer does something nobody predicted in the strategy.

Unilever chose a different path. For years, the company has been one of the best-known examples of digitalised, AI-assisted marketing recruitment, but its current description of the process emphasises precisely the combination of technology and human contact. A large part of behavioural hiring technology tries to infer personality from behaviour. Unilever's process, at least according to its own account, uses technology more to structure the selection than to construct a candidate from digital traces.

In other words, AI doesn't have to play psychologist to be useful.

It can remove the admin burden, and recruiters who don't have 30 seconds to read someone's application. Find profiles. Compare experience. Flag gaps in a CV that a human missed. Prepare questions. It can standardise part of the assessment. But after that, someone still has to talk to the candidate, ask a question that wasn't in the script, hear an answer that isn't perfectly structured, and judge something that can't be reduced to how long someone held eye contact, how many pauses they took, or how they smiled in the first seven seconds.

That's an important difference. And it may be the more mature way of applying AI.

Onboarding begins once we stop selling each other perception

The same thing applies after the candidate is chosen. In my experience, most of the problems with the quality of hiring in marketing rarely came from a seven-page CV, or even from the whole process leading up to the hiring decision. Much more often, the real trouble starts only afterwards — once we've chosen the candidate and then hand them over to bad onboarding.

AI can put together the onboarding schedule, explain procedures, and answer 150 FAQs after the 58th PowerPoint presentation the new brand manager dutifully read and that nobody else has opened since last year's annual meeting. It can personalise training, send reminders, measure progress, and make sure nobody skips module number 17.

But the brand manager has to get to know the brand's memory.

Why a certain line extension failed. Why the distributor hates a particular pack. Why sales doesn't trust the new project. Why a product that looks successful is actually a strategic problem. Why a campaign everyone now considers brilliant was almost cancelled at the start. And why a particular mistake in that campaign has now been made for the fourth time.

That gets passed on by people. The organisation has to prove it's good enough that people want to stay in it. Some companies, for that reason, pair the formal onboarding with a buddy assigned to the new employee, to help them understand the organisation and its culture.

What if the problem isn't the candidate?

So I don't think we should stop teaching brand managers how to present themselves. A good CV is better than a bad one. A clear answer is better than mumbling. And proof is better than adjectives.

But everyone has perception. The problem starts when the impression of expertise becomes the proof of expertise.

If good perception were proof of a good hiring decision, such a system should be producing incredibly good results.

But companies should stop asking candidates to compensate for their own bad selection criteria. The organisation has to adapt to the new employee. And create conditions where people can progress long-term, be authentic, and be sustainable.

AI can find a candidate who perfectly matches your perception of a good brand manager.

The real question is far more uncomfortable:

What if the problem is your perception itself?

It's easiest to fix the CV. It's much more uncomfortable to ask whether the problem is in the system reading it.

The behavioural approach asks: How can the candidate send a better signal and perception? Political economy asks: Who decides what counts as a good signal? Who holds the power to choose? How is the job designed? How much does it pay? How many people leave? Why do they leave? Who bears the consequences of a bad hire?

W. Edwards Deming put it much more simply: "A bad system will beat a good person every time."

Every recruiter should tape that next to their monitor.

And every director above them.

So a brand manager shouldn't be chosen like a brand. A brand manager has to have expertise. They have to understand the customer, the P&L, the product, pricing, distribution and communication. They have to tell insight apart from data, innovation apart from mere novelty, and brand growth apart from short-term volume. They have to know how to collaborate. But they also have to know how to say no sometimes. Especially to their own boss.

AI can find a brand manager who perfectly matches your LinkedIn ad.

The brand manager you actually need might be exactly the one who, on day one, explains to you what was wrong with that ad.

What AI can do, and what the brand manager must do:

AI can write the job ad. The brand manager has to define the job. AI can process applications. The brand manager has to define the criteria. AI can find patterns. The brand manager has to recognise the exception. AI can speed up onboarding. The brand manager has to pass on the brand's memory.
